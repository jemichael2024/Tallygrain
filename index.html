Particle sizing is still done by hand, and it shows.

Every nanomaterials lab report, thesis chapter and paper needs a size distribution. The usual route is FIJI: draw a line across each particle, record it, repeat a few hundred times. Free automated options exist, but they take tuning that most people never get around to.

200

particles is the rule-of-thumb minimum for a statistically meaningful size estimate. An experienced researcher sizes about that many by hand in an hour.

≈40

samples per work week is the ceiling for manual sizing at that pace, and two people measuring the same image by hand will almost certainly get different results.

Source: Aviles & Lear, “Practical Guide to Automated TEM Image Analysis…”, ACS Nanoscience Au, 2025.

How it works

Step 1

Drop the image

Any SEM or TEM export: TIFF, PNG or JPG. Crop off the instrument data bar with one setting.

Step 2

Set the scale

Type the scale bar length in nm and in pixels. Planned: read the bar automatically.

Step 3

Check and export

Every particle is outlined so you can see what was counted. Copy the CSV or the histogram straight into your report.

Known limits, stated up front

  • Touching or overlapping particles are flagged and excluded, which biases counts on dense samples.
  • Low-contrast TEM images or uneven backgrounds can fool a single global threshold.
  • Equivalent circular diameter suits near-spherical particles. Rods and plates need length and width, which is not built yet.
